# Digital Gardening vs. Chronological Blogs 🌿

For twenty years, the dominant metaphor of the web has been the **stream**: chronological feeds, timeline updates, tweets, and reverse-chronological blog posts where yesterday's thoughts are pushed off the page to die.

A **Digital Garden**, popularized by thinkers like Maggie Appleton, offers a radical alternative: **spatial, interconnected, living knowledge**.

## The Core Differences

| Traditional Blog | Digital Garden |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Chronological stream** (latest post on top) | **Topological web** (organized by folders & links) |
| **Finished essays** published once | **Living notes** cultivated and revised over time |
| **Monolithic posts** in isolation | **Atomic notes** linked via [[wikilinks]] |
| **Date-centric** archive | **Topic-centric** evergreen repository |

> [!info] The Garden Metaphor
> In a garden, you plant seeds (quick notes, rough drafts), water them (revisiting and expanding), prune weeds, and connect pathways. Some plants grow into sturdy oak trees; others remain delicate wildflowers.

## Why Folder Hierarchy Matters
While pure tag clouds quickly become chaotic, a thoughtful folder structure provides **contextual warmth**:

- \`journal/\` for dated personal logs and daily reflections
- \`thoughts/\` for evolving essays and original ideas
- \`reading-notes/\` for summaries like [[reading-notes/atomic-habits-takeaways|Atomic Habits]]
- \`guides/\` for tutorials like [[guides/hosting-on-github-and-cloudflare|Hosting on Cloudflare]]
- Root files (like [[about|About Me]]) for standalone pages

When your notes are organized in natural folders, anyone browsing your garden can navigate the landscape with clarity.
